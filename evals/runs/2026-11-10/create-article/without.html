The Weight of Light

At a former salt warehouse on a northern estuary, a small studio asks whether a wall can be thick enough to hold the day inside it.

Words Ines Halvorsen Drawings Tomas Ekwueme Reading time 7 min

You arrive by the sea wall, and for a long while the building does not seem to want you. It is a brick box, windowless on the estuary side, seven bays long, the colour of old rust. Gulls use the ridge. Only when the path turns inland do the openings appear, and they are not what you expect: not glass but long, tapering cavities in the masonry, each wide as a doorway at the inner face and narrowing to a slot outside.

The Salt House was built in 1894 to store a commodity that dissolves in damp air, and so its first architects solved for dryness, not delight. The walls are more than a metre through. The studio that converted it, a five-person practice working from the next town up the coast, decided early on that this thickness was the building’s only real asset, and that every intervention should be measured against it.

A wall you can stand in

“We kept asking what the wall was for,” says the practice’s co-founder, Dara Okonkwo-Lind, as she steps into one of the reveals and sits on the sill, her shoulders almost touching both sides. “Originally, to keep water out. Now, I think, to slow light down.”

The idea is simple to state and difficult to build. Daylight at this latitude arrives low and sideways for much of the year. In a conventional glazed box it floods in, flattens the room and leaves by evening. Here it has to be coaxed along the reveal, bouncing off lime-washed splays, losing intensity at each contact, until by the time it reaches the floor it has the soft, directional quality of light in a church or a cellar.

A thin wall shows you the weather. A thick one lets you watch the weather arrive. Dara Okonkwo-Lind, co-founder

The team modelled the reveals through more than two hundred iterations, adjusting the angle of each splay by a few degrees to shift where a patch of sun would land at noon on the solstices. The final geometry is not symmetrical. The western openings are deeper and steeper, to temper the late glare; the eastern ones open wider, so that morning reaches the long communal table by the second hour after sunrise.

What the building costs to keep

There is an honest accounting of compromise here. The thick walls are cold in winter and the studio has resisted insulating them from the inside, which would have destroyed the reveals. Instead the heating is carried in the floor, a slab of recycled brick laid over a bed of crushed shell, and the occupants wear wool. Visitors are warned. Some leave unconvinced.

Others find the discomfort part of the argument. A building that asks something of you, the architects contend, makes you attentive to it, and attention is a kind of care. The roof, a lightweight timber structure on slender steel ties, rests on the old walls so quietly that from inside it seems to hover. Nothing new touches the brick except where it must.

Afternoon, in the long room

By three o’clock in October the western light has found the far end of the main hall. It does not fall as a rectangle, as it would from a window, but as a tall, wavering lozenge, softened at the edges where it has touched the splays. Someone has chalked its position on the floor over several weeks, and the marks drift like a slow clock.

It would be easy to call this nostalgia, a retreat into mass at a moment when the industry prizes lightness and speed. But the Salt House is not a lament. It is a patient demonstration that old materials, read carefully, can still teach a design problem its terms. The warehouse was built to keep the sea out. It now keeps the day in, a little longer than it should, and the room is better for it. ■

The Salt House is open to visitors on the first weekend of each month. Guided tours of the archive begin at eleven.
