Sixty Centimetres of Wall

Words Ines Valmar
Drawings Marrow & Quay

In a small northern town, a municipal archive has been built with walls thick enough to keep the records safe and the building free of machines. It asks what a wall is for.

The first thing the visitor does at the Halden Archive is put a hand on the building. Nobody is told to. The reveals at the entrance are deep enough to lean into, the brick is cool even in the afternoon, and the door seems to be cut out of the wall, not hung in front of it.

At a glanceHalden Municipal Archive, Orsolt. Marrow & Quay. Opened spring 2026.

That is the argument of the building, and it is made with a single dimension. The outer walls are sixty centimetres thick. Everything else, from the plan to the way the reading room is lit, follows from the decision to let a wall do the work that most archives hand to a plant room.

Paper keeps best when its surroundings change slowly. A conventional archive achieves this with sensors, ducts and a standing maintenance contract. Here the town's brief asked for something that would still work if the contract lapsed. The architects answered with mass: a heavy, breathing envelope that takes a day to feel a cold night and another to forget it.

Brief, 2021Keep the collection stable without relying on continuous mechanical control. The studio treated the sentence as a structural requirement.

§1

The wall as a slow instrument

Read the section from the outside in. A skin of fired brick takes the weather. Behind it a channel only two centimetres wide is left open, so that air can rise through the wall and leave beneath the eaves. Behind that sits hollow clay block, plastered in lime, which absorbs moisture when the room is damp and returns it when the room is dry.

None of this is new. Every layer would be recognised by a mason of the last century. What is unusual is that the layers have been left to be the whole system, with no membrane or mechanical backup standing by to forgive a miscalculation. The drawing is unforgiving, and the architects say they liked that about it.

§2

A room that changes its mind slowly

Inside, the effect is difficult to photograph and easy to feel. Stand in the reading room on a bright morning and the light on the plaster is soft and even, because the window reveals are deep enough to be rooms of their own. Sit by the window and you notice the sill is wide enough for a book, a cup, an elbow. The thickness has been put to use.

There are costs, and the architects list them without being asked. The footprint is larger for the same floor area. The window openings are fewer and each one is a piece of joinery. The building cannot be adjusted by turning a dial, so it has to be run by people who understand it, and the archivists have written a short manual that begins with how to open a window.

Trade-offThick walls consume plan area. The studio estimates the loss and accepts it; the figure here is left for the real feature.

Whether the approach travels is the question the profession will argue over. It suits a cool climate, a modest collection and a client willing to be patient. It would be foolish to apply it to a glass-fronted tower. But as a rebuke to the idea that every building must be an appliance, it is the clearest argument made in brick in some years.
