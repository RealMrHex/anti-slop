The Wall That Breathes

A municipal bathhouse spent ninety years sealing water out. Its restorers spent four trying to let it back in.

Words Placeholder Author Drawings Placeholder Studio 21 September 2026 · 9 min read

The first thing the surveyors noticed at the Hallgate Baths was the smell, and the second was that it was coming from the repairs. Every patch laid since 1962 was cement: hard, grey, and faintly warm to the hand. Around each one the original brick had crumbled to the colour and texture of brown sugar.

The diagnosis was not mysterious. A masonry wall is not a barrier; it is a slow conversation between wet and dry. Water enters as rain, rises from the ground, condenses from the air of a building full of hot pools, and leaves again by evaporating through whatever surface will let it. Close that surface with something dense, and the water does not stop arriving. It simply chooses another way out, and the brick, being softer than the cement, pays.

1A soft skin on a hard problem

Lime plaster is porous, weak and slow. It cures by taking carbon dioxide back from the air, which takes weeks, and it cracks if it is rushed. For most of the twentieth century these were reasons to abandon it. For the conservators at Hallgate they were the specification: a skin weaker than the wall beneath it, so that when something has to give, it is the cheap, replaceable layer that does.

Cure time. The east hall was plastered in three thin coats, each left to dry before the next. The sequence took eleven weeks.

The team ran the building like a long experiment. They removed the cement in sections, logged the condition of the brick behind each, and let the exposed walls dry for a full season before touching them. Moisture readings fell steadily. Nobody on site found this exciting, and that, one of them told me, was the point.

2What a building is allowed to do

The usual language of restoration is the language of return: to a date, to a drawing, to a photograph. Hallgate's team distrusted it. The 1932 building was never in a stable state; it was always drying, always taking on water, always being mended. What they chose to restore was not an appearance but a behaviour.

Method. Readings were taken weekly at the same twelve points, at the same hour, by the same person.

“We stopped asking what the wall should look like and started asking what it should be allowed to do.”

Conservation lead, Hallgate Baths (placeholder quotation)

That shift has costs. A lime wall wants repainting in lime wash every few years; it marks, it stains, it shows its age honestly and unevenly. The council, which pays for upkeep, had to be persuaded that visible weathering was a sign of a healthy wall rather than a neglected one. The compromise was a maintenance calendar pinned inside the boiler room door, where the people who actually do the work will see it.

3Slow work, short season

On the day I visited, the pools had been refilled and the hall was loud with the ordinary noise of a public building working: whistles, shoes, a child refusing to get out. The east wall ran in a pale, uneven band behind the lifeguard’s chair. Close to, it was slightly damp to the touch, and cooler than the air.

That coolness is the wall working. It is the sound, as far as a wall has one, of water leaving.
