The Last Letterpress on Calder Street

For sixty-one years the Okafor family has set type by hand in the same narrow shop. The lease ends in March.

By Ines Marchetti · Photographs by Tom Avery · 14 minute read

The first thing you notice is the sound, a slow iron clap every four seconds, and then the smell of linseed and solvent. Adaeze Okafor does not look up when the door opens. She is locking a forme, and a forme will not wait.

Her grandfather bought the Heidelberg platen second-hand in 1964. It has printed wedding invitations for three generations of the same families, ballots for a union that no longer exists, and, for one strange summer, every menu on the street.

A trade measured in points

Letterpress rewards patience and punishes haste. A line set a hair too loose will work itself free on the two-hundredth impression. Okafor checks each one with her thumbnail.

You can’t undo metal. You can only set it again, properly.

The building’s new owners have offered the shop a unit in a retail park eleven miles away. The press weighs just over a tonne. The floor there, she says, is not rated for it.
